# Data Exploration

Explore data to understand their structure, coverage, quality, and implications for design. Keep discovery separate from confirmatory estimation and record decisions generated by exploration.

## Step 1: Select the mode

Choose one or combine explicitly:

- `general`: cross-sectional or tabular inventory;
- `panel`: units observed across time;
- `temporal`: time series, irregular dates, seasonality, or structural breaks;
- `spatial`: geographic coverage, boundaries, clustering, and neighborhood structure;
- `design-aware`: feasibility checks for a selected causal or structural design.

State whether the analysis occurs before or after strategy selection. Do not use post-treatment outcome exploration to choose a preferred causal specification without disclosure.

## Step 2: Audit provenance and measurement status

For each dataset, record:

- source and acquisition version;
- raw, interim, clean, or derived status;
- unit of observation and expected key;
- geographic, temporal, population, and sectoral coverage;
- weights, denominators, and aggregation level;
- source-criticism and measurement artifacts available;
- scripts or transformations already applied.

Activate `research-historical-source-criticism` when production or coverage is unclear. Activate `research-historical-measurement` when definitions, units, categories, boundaries, or denominators may not be comparable.

## Step 3: Validate structure and coverage

Report:

- rows, columns, types, labels, and storage classes;
- duplicate and conflicting keys;
- observed and expected unit-time or geography-time cells;
- entry, exit, gaps, and panel balance;
- spatial and temporal coverage maps or tables;
- sampling or analytical weights and effective support;
- coverage by relevant groups, periods, treatment cohorts, or source regimes.

Do not interpret an unbalanced or changing sample as a stable population without evidence.

## Step 4: Diagnose missingness

Distinguish zero, not applicable, unrecorded, illegible, censored, suppressed, structurally missing, and ordinary missing values where metadata permit.

Profile missingness by variable, time, place, source, treatment status, and relevant group. Examine monotone attrition, co-missingness, and whether missingness changes the target population. Do not impute during EDA unless the task is explicitly to compare imputation strategies.

## Step 5: Profile variables

For numeric variables, report distribution, quantiles, dispersion, zeros, bounds, heaping, rounding, censoring, and weighted summaries when appropriate. For categorical variables, report levels, rare categories, changes over time, and unmapped values. For dates, report range, gaps, irregular intervals, and reference-period ambiguity.

Always include units, denominator, price basis, transformation, and source regime when known. Separate original, harmonized, and derived variables.

## Step 6: Examine structure relevant to the question

Select analyses by design rather than exhaustively correlating all variables:

- trends, breaks, cohort composition, and event timing;
- within- and between-unit variation;
- weighted and unweighted group comparisons;
- spatial concentration, coverage, and neighbor dependence;
- distributions and heterogeneity hidden by means;
- relationships implied by hypotheses or mechanisms;
- support and overlap for candidate comparisons.

Label all relationships as descriptive. Avoid automated significance testing across large numbers of exploratory comparisons.

## Step 7: Diagnose anomalies and discontinuities

Investigate:

- impossible or implausible values;
- spikes, breaks, flat runs, and duplicated series;
- digit preference and transcription patterns;
- unit, currency, category, boundary, or source changes;
- changes aligned with administrative capacity rather than the historical process;
- influential units, periods, cohorts, or regions.

Do not remove anomalies during exploration. Classify them as error, historical event, definition change, unresolved, or valid extreme, and route the decision to preparation or measurement.

## Step 8: Record implications without overclaiming

Separate:

- established data facts;
- provisional substantive patterns;
- measurement or source concerns;
- hypotheses suggested or weakened;
- design feasibility implications;
- decisions requiring author confirmation;
- confirmatory tests reserved for later stages.

If exploration changes a hypothesis, outcome, treatment, sample, exposure window, or main specification, record the change in the project log.

## R conventions

- Read `project/project_profile.yaml` and `../shared/r_code_style.md`.
- Apply the shared readable-transformation and complexity rules. Keep coverage,
  sample selection, and descriptive calculations visible in the main script;
  consult `../shared/r_code_examples.md` for an aggregation with declared support.
- Use explicit package namespaces in reusable code.
- Use `|>`, tidyverse verbs, `.by`, and `across()` where appropriate.
- Use `ggplot2` and `implementation-figures` for final-quality figures.
- Set weights, missing-value handling, and denominators explicitly.
- Do not use `geom_smooth()` or automated correlation matrices as substitutes for a design-relevant question.
- Run and record required `styler` and `lintr` checks; `not assessed` is not a pass.

## Rules

- Preserve exploratory-confirmatory boundaries.
- Never silently drop missing, duplicate, or extreme observations.
- Never infer measurement stability from a stable column name.
- Never compare means across changing populations without composition checks.
- Report support and coverage before interpreting relationships.
- Return source and measurement problems to their responsible skills.
