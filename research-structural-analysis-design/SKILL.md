# Skill: Historical-Structural Analysis Design

You design papers that explain historical transformations without requiring econometric causal identification as the primary standard of evidence.

This skill is for structural, historical, environmental, agrarian, and ecological-economic papers where the central contribution is an explanation of a process rather than a single identified effect.

## Core principle

Historical-structural analysis is not mere narrative. It must connect patterns, mechanisms, actors, institutions, scales, and evidence into a coherent explanation.

## When to use

Use this skill when the project studies:
- long-run structural change,
- agrarian transformation,
- commodity frontier expansion,
- environmental or metabolic transitions,
- institutional change,
- unequal ecological exchange,
- development pathways,
- land, labor, state, and environmental interactions.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Define the structural question
Ask:
- What historical process must be explained?
- What changes over time?
- What remains continuous?
- Why is the process analytically important?

### Step 2: Establish periodization
Define:
- start and end points,
- sub-periods,
- structural breaks,
- lags,
- historical transitions.

Avoid treating long periods as homogeneous.

### Step 3: Define scale
Specify:
- local,
- regional,
- national,
- global,
- commodity-chain,
- ecological or metabolic scale.

Explain how scales connect.

### Step 4: Identify core patterns
List the empirical patterns the paper must establish:
- trends,
- breaks,
- spatial differences,
- compositional shifts,
- institutional changes,
- environmental indicators,
- distributional changes.

### Step 5: Propose mechanisms
For each pattern, define:
- actors,
- institutions,
- incentives,
- constraints,
- environmental mediations,
- political-economic dynamics.

### Step 6: Specify evidence
Identify what evidence is needed:
- quantitative indicators,
- archival sources,
- secondary literature,
- maps,
- commodity statistics,
- land records,
- state reports,
- environmental accounts.

Use `research-historical-source-criticism` to evaluate how the required evidence was produced, selected, preserved, and transformed. Use `research-historical-measurement` when patterns depend on indicators, harmonized series, classifications, boundaries, prices, or biophysical conversions.

### Step 7: Define competing interpretations
For each mechanism, state at least one rival explanation.

### Step 8: State limits of inference
Clarify what the design can and cannot establish.

### Step 9: Produce the implementation handoff

Activate `implementation-historical-structural-analysis` after acquisition, source criticism, measurement, and preparation. Provide:

```markdown
## Historical-Structural Implementation Handoff
- Structural question and process:
- Proposed periodization and alternative boundaries:
- Units, cases, comparisons, and connected scales:
- Core temporal, spatial, compositional, institutional, or socioecological patterns:
- Mechanism sequences, actors, institutions, and mediations:
- Rival interpretations and discriminating evidence:
- Required sources, indicators, locators, and source-production risks:
- Measurement, boundary, classification, price, and aggregation decisions:
- Evidence expected to establish patterns versus mechanisms:
- Feasibility thresholds, claims allowed, and claims not allowed:
```

Do not choose periods, comparisons, or evidence because they yield a preferred narrative. Mark every unresolved design decision for feasibility assessment.

## Quality criteria

A strong structural design:
- has a clear process to explain,
- uses explicit periodization,
- links scales coherently,
- defines mechanisms rather than only trends,
- identifies actors and institutions,
- distinguishes evidence from interpretation,
- acknowledges alternative explanations.

## Do not

- Do not treat historical-structural analysis as background narrative.
- Do not equate descriptive statistics with explanation.
- Do not make causal claims without clarifying inference limits.
- Do not flatten regional or temporal heterogeneity.
- Do not detach environmental indicators from political economy.
